The Apophis 2029 Asteroid and Golden Pi: Could a π Error Change Earth's Fate?

May 19, 2026

The Warning

On January 29, 2018, a letter was sent to the White House that, if its premise is correct, carries implications far beyond mathematical curiosity. Harry Lear — an independent researcher who has spent years investigating the true value of π — addressed an open letter to President Donald Trump, warning that NASA's trajectory calculations for the near-Earth asteroid Apophis (99942 Apophis) are fundamentally flawed.

The error, according to Lear, is not in NASA's computers or orbital models. The error is in the value of π itself.

Apophis gained global attention in 2004 when astronomers calculated a 2.7% chance of impact during its planned 2029 flyby. Subsequent refinements ruled out a collision, and NASA now projects the 340-meter asteroid will pass within approximately 31,000 kilometers of Earth on April 13, 2029 — closer than many geostationary satellites, but safe. Lear's claim is that this "safe" conclusion rests on calculations using the conventional value of π (3.14159...), and that using the correct value — golden π = 4/√φ = 3.144606 — changes the outcome dramatically.

The Scale of the Discrepancy

Lear calculated the difference in orbital circumference for both Earth and Apophis when computed with golden π versus conventional π. His results:

  • Earth's orbital circumference difference: ~901,434 km
  • Apophis's orbital circumference difference: ~831,517 km

To put this in perspective: the combined discrepancy in orbital path length over a single orbit exceeds 1.7 million kilometers. Apophis's projected closest approach in 2029 is roughly 31,000 km — a value nearly 30 times smaller than the claimed π-induced error margin. If Lear is right, the difference is not merely academic; it is large enough to transform a near-miss into a direct impact.

The Physics: Is an Orbital Circumference the Right Test?

This is the central point of contention. Critics of Lear's analysis observe that orbital mechanics are not computed using the simple formula C = πd. NASA's trajectory models rely on Kepler's laws of planetary motion and Newtonian gravitation, integrated numerically through n-body simulations. These methods do not multiply a diameter by π anywhere in their core logic. Instead, they solve differential equations of gravitational attraction — equations in which π appears only indirectly (for instance, in gravitational constants derived from orbital periods and distances).

Lear's counterargument is that π enters orbital mechanics at a deeper level. The gravitational constant G and related astronomical parameters are empirically calibrated against observed orbital data. If those calibrations were performed using conventional π, any subsequent derivation that reintroduces π — such as converting between orbital period and semi-major axis, or computing positional predictions from angular parameters — inherits the π error. The claim is not that NASA explicitly multiplies diameters by π, but that π is embedded in the foundational constants and conversion factors used throughout the calculations.

This is where the debate becomes technically dense. Does a 0.096% difference in π (the gap between 3.14159 and 3.144606) propagate through the entire chain of orbital mechanics at a scale sufficient to alter an asteroid's predicted position by hundreds of thousands of kilometers over years of simulation? Lear says yes. Mainstream physics says no.

Golden Pi: What It Is and Where It Comes From

For those unfamiliar with the term: golden π (also called "true π") is the value π = 4/√φ = 3.144605..., derived from the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2 ≈ 1.618033. Unlike conventional π, which is transcendental (cannot be expressed as a finite algebraic formula), golden π is constructible with compass and straightedge, emerging naturally from the geometry of the golden ratio.

Three independent physical experiments over seven years have measured golden π directly:

  • CNC machining test (2014): A precision-machined disk measured with 0.0001 mm resolution, yielding π ≈ 3.1446
  • Laser measurement test (2014): A laser micrometer measuring a rotating cylinder over 200+ test runs, converging on golden π
  • Rolling test (2021): A precision-ground cylinder rolled across a measured surface, independently reproducing the golden π result

These experiments were conducted with measurement precision roughly 1,000 times greater than the 0.096% gap between golden π and conventional π, making systematic error an increasingly strained explanation for the deviation.

The FIGU and TheyFly Connection

The golden π research has been endorsed by FIGU (Freie Interessengemeinschaft für Grenz- und Geisteswissenschaften und Ufologiestudien), the organization associated with Billy Meier. Contact Report 856 states: "3.1446 are correct. However, what follows after 6 remains unknown." This confirmation of the first five digits lends the claim a significant endorsement within certain circles.

The TheyFly blog, which has extensively documented the golden π research and the Apophis warnings, has been a primary outlet for Lear's analysis outside of academic channels. The blog has published detailed breakdowns of the orbital circumference calculations and the underlying mathematics, and has connected the Apophis warning to broader questions about the foundations of mathematics and physics.

The Broader Stakes

Even if Lear's orbital analysis is wrong, the underlying question remains: Is π = 4/√φ the true geometric π? The three physical experiments suggest that the conventional value may be off by approximately 0.3%. In everyday engineering, this margin is negligible. In precision applications — satellite positioning, long-term orbital prediction, interstellar navigation — it could become significant.

Consider what is at stake if golden π is correct and conventional π is wrong:

  • Every circular geometry in existing engineering — gears, bearings, turbines, engines — has been designed with the wrong constant.
  • GPS satellite triangulation, which depends on relativistic corrections and orbital parameters, could contain systematic positional biases.
  • The fine-structure constant (α ≈ 1/137), already shown to relate to 432 and φ in certain formulations, may align perfectly when calculated with golden π.
  • Asteroid and comet trajectory predictions — including Apophis — would need recalculation.

The Skeptic's View

It is important to present the counterarguments in good faith. The overwhelming consensus among physicists and mathematicians is that:

  • π has been proven transcendental (Lindemann–Weierstrass, 1882), meaning it cannot equal an algebraic expression like 4/√φ.
  • The physical experiments that measured golden π may have systematic errors in circumference or diameter measurement (e.g., thermal expansion, micrometer calibration, non-ideal cylinders).
  • Orbital mechanics do not compute positions via C = πd, and the propagation of a π error through the n-body integration of gravitational equations would not produce Lear's claimed ~900,000 km discrepancy.
  • Apophis has been tracked through hundreds of optical and radar observations over two decades, and its orbit is constrained to within a few kilometers at the epoch of its 2029 flyby.

These are not trivial objections. For golden π to be correct, a significant portion of modern mathematics and physics would need revision. The claim of a wrong π is a claim about an error at the very foundation of geometry — and extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.

Why This Matters Now

With Apophis's 2029 flyby now just under three years away, the window for independent verification is closing. If Lear is correct, the consequences of inaction are catastrophic. If Lear is incorrect, the hypothesis can be conclusively falsified by direct observation when Apophis passes Earth.

There is a pragmatic argument for independent verification regardless of one's position: the path to disproving golden π is the same as the path to confirming it. A dedicated recalculation of Apophis's trajectory using both values of π, documented transparently and compared against the ever-improving observational data, would settle the question either way. The cost is computational time. The benefit is certainty — and perhaps, the resolution of one of the most consequential mathematical debates in history.

April 13, 2029 will arrive regardless. The question is whether we are using the right numbers to know what will happen when it does.
