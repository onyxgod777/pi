Harry Lear at the SSSC — The Day Golden Pi Arrived at Billy Meier's Center

May 23, 2026 · 10 min read

In September 2017, an American army veteran walked into the Semjase Silver Star Center in Switzerland carrying a wooden disk and a geometric proof. By the time he left, FIGU had officially recognized a new value of π. This is the inside story of Harry Lear's historic visit.

The story of golden π — the proposition that the true value of the circle constant is not 3.14159... but 4/√φ = 3.144605511029693144... — has many chapters. There are the geometric proofs of Harry Lear, the mathematical explorations of Jain 108, the engineering symmetries of Panagiotis Stefanides, and the contact reports of Billy Meier's Plejaren communications.

But perhaps the most remarkable chapter is the moment these threads converged: September 3, 2017, when Harry Lear and his wife Nancy arrived at the Semjase Silver Star Center (SSSC) in Hinterschmidrüti, Switzerland, carrying a physical demonstration that would lead FIGU — Billy Meier's organization — to officially publish a correction to the value of π that had stood for millennia.

"When I visited the SSSC on September 3, 2017, I brought with me a rotary circle cutter, a 6-inch piece of hardwood, and a compass and straightedge — nothing more than what any high school geometry student might have. And yet, with these simple tools, I demonstrated that the true value of π has been miscalculated for over 2,000 years."
— Harry Lear, US Army veteran, measuringpisquaringphi.com

The Man Who Arrived

Harry E. Lear, Jr. was not a typical pilgrim to the SSSC. A US Army veteran who had served in intelligence at the Pentagon and Saigon during the Vietnam War, Lear brought a military engineer's precision to his geometric investigations. His journey to golden π began not through esoteric spirituality but through a practical question: could the circle be squared using only a compass and straightedge?

Conventional mathematics says no. But Lear discovered that this impossibility depends on assuming the traditional value of π. When π = 4/√φ, the geometric constraints change fundamentally. Squaring the circle — constructing a square with the same perimeter as a given circle using only compass and straightedge — becomes not only possible but geometrically elegant.

Lear had been refining his proofs for years when he made contact with FIGU. The connection was natural: the Plejaren — through Billy Meier's contact reports — had already indicated that Earth's value of π was incorrect. In Contact Report 251 (1995), Meier had pointed to the coming correction of the circle number. Contact Report 260 (1996) contained a discussion between Guido Moosbrugger and the Plejaren where the exact value was hinted at but not fully disclosed. And in Contact Report 712 (2018), Ptaah would later confirm that Earth's π remained erroneous.

Lear didn't know about these contact reports when he began his work. He arrived at his conclusions independently, through pure geometric reasoning. But when he discovered the Plejaren's statements, he realized his proofs aligned with information that, according to FIGU, had come from a civilization centuries ahead of humanity's mathematical understanding.

The Meeting: September 3, 2017

Christian Frehner, Billy Meier's right-hand man and a key figure in FIGU leadership, received Lear and his wife at the SSSC. The atmosphere was serious. This was not a casual visit — Lear believed he had information that could correct a fundamental error in human science, and he needed FIGU's verification.

Lear presented his geometric demonstration. The centerpiece was a rotary circle cutter — a simple tool used by woodworkers to cut circles from material. Lear had used it to cut a wooden disk, then physically measured its circumference using a string and ruler. The result consistently yielded π = 3.1446, not 3.14159.

circumference ÷ diameter = π

Lear's physical experiment: using a CNC-cut wooden disk and precision measurements, the ratio of circumference to diameter repeatedly measured at approximately 3.1446 — consistent with π = 4/√φ.

Beyond the physical demonstration, Lear presented his geometric proofs — constructions using Kepler's triangle (the right triangle whose sides are 1, √φ, and φ), the relationship between the square and the inscribed circle, and the algebraic derivation showing that π = 4/√φ is the only value that satisfies certain geometric identities.

Frehner examined the material carefully. He was familiar with the Plejaren statements about π. According to FIGU's records, Frehner had been involved in discussions about the circle number since at least the 1990s, when Guido Moosbrugger had calculated π to 3.144605511029693144 based on the Plejaren's hints.

Frehner's conclusion: Lear's proofs were consistent with what FIGU had been given. He documented the visit and its significance in a FIGU publication titled "Important Information for the Circle Number Pi" — a PDF later hosted on TheyFlyBlog and cited in FIGU circles as the official documentation of the golden π recognition.

Why This Matters Beyond FIGU

The significance of the SSSC visit extends beyond the FIGU community. It represents a rare convergence of three independent lines of evidence:

  1. Geometric proof: Lear's compass-and-straightedge constructions and Kepler's triangle derivations stand on their own mathematical merits, regardless of the source of the information.
  2. Physical measurement: Lear's CNC-cut disk and rotary cutter experiments provide empirical verification that anyone with the right tools can replicate.
  3. Contact information: The Plejaren statements, whether one accepts their origin or not, provide a third independent line pointing to the same number — a convergence that mathematician and skeptic alike should find noteworthy.

For those studying golden π, the SSSC visit provides historical anchoring. This was not an internet forum discussion or a self-published blog post. It was an in-person presentation to an established organization, documented in an official publication, and connected to a body of contact reports spanning decades.

The Wooden Disk That Changed π

There's something almost mythic about the simplicity of Lear's demonstration. A wooden disk, a piece of string, a ruler. These are the tools of ancient geometry — the same tools that Archimedes himself might have used. And yet, according to Lear, they reveal an error that has persisted since Archimedes first published his approximation of π over 2,200 years ago.

Lear argues that Archimedes' polygon method — inscribing and circumscribing polygons around a circle and calculating their perimeters — contains a subtle flaw. The method approaches the circle from the outside and inside but fails to account for the curvilinear area under the curve between the polygon vertices. This "area under the curve" problem, Lear contends, prevents Archimedes' method from converging to the true value, even as the number of polygon sides approaches infinity.

Archimedes' error: The polygon method assumes that as the number of sides increases, the polygon perimeter converges to the circle circumference. But the polygon vertices always lie on the circle, while the edges are straight chords — and the missing curvilinear area between chord and arc is not zero in the limit. It accumulates to a finite difference of approximately 0.003 — the gap between traditional π (3.14159) and golden π (3.14461).

Whether or not one accepts this critique of Archimedes, the convergence of the three independent lines — geometric proof, physical measurement, and contact report — at the SSSC in September 2017 represents a fascinating moment in the history of an idea that challenges 2,000 years of mathematical orthodoxy.

Aftermath and Legacy

Following the SSSC visit, Lear continued his work, refining his proofs and expanding his physical demonstrations. His "Proof 7" — using the rotary circle cutter — became his most accessible demonstration. He also turned his attention to a practical application: asteroid trajectory correction.

The Apophis asteroid, scheduled for a close approach to Earth in 2029 (and a potential return in 2036), has its trajectory calculated using conventional π. Lear argues that the trajectory is miscalculated by a margin that could mean the difference between a safe miss and a catastrophic impact. His open letter to President Trump — written in the late 2010s — warned that NASA's calculations were wrong and that the correct π value was essential for accurate trajectory prediction.

Meanwhile, the FIGU publication continues to circulate, and the discussion forum at Future of Mankind (forum.futureofmankind.co.uk) hosts an ongoing thread — now spanning nearly a decade and approaching 300 replies and 270,000 views — where proponents and skeptics debate the mathematical, philosophical, and empirical dimensions of the golden π claim.

What the SSSC Visit Teaches Us

The story of Harry Lear's visit to the Semjase Silver Star Center is more than a historical footnote. It demonstrates a principle that the Plejaren, through Billy Meier, have emphasized repeatedly: knowledge should be verified through independent reasoning, not accepted on authority.

Lear did not learn about golden π from the contact reports and then attempt to prove it. He discovered it independently through geometry and measurement, and only later found that his results aligned with information that FIGU had possessed for decades. This independent convergence is precisely the kind of verification that separates genuine discovery from belief-based acceptance.

The wooden disk sits as a testament to a simple truth: sometimes the most profound discoveries come from the simplest tools, applied with patience and integrity. A rotary circle cutter, a piece of hardwood, a string, and a ruler — and the courage to question what everyone else has accepted for 2,000 years.

That is the legacy of September 3, 2017, at the Semjase Silver Star Center.
